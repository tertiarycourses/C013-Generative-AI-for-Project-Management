## Introduction

This Learner Guide accompanies the course Generative AI for Project Management (C013), conducted by Tertiary Infotech Academy Pte Ltd. It provides detailed step-by-step instructions for all 25 hands-on activities, organised by the three course topics and mapped to the three learning outcomes.

Every activity in this course is GenAI-first. You will prompt a Generative AI tool to produce a first-pass project artifact — a charter, a work breakdown structure, a risk register, a schedule — and then critically review, correct and complete it. That review step is the skill this course teaches. GenAI drafts; you decide.

Use this guide alongside the course slides. The slides show each activity's goal, the prompt and the artifact you produce; this guide carries the full step-by-step detail you follow at your own pace.

## Before You Start — Your GenAI Toolkit

**What you need**

- A laptop with a modern web browser and internet access.
- An account on at least one Generative AI tool — ChatGPT (chat.openai.com), Claude (claude.ai), Google Gemini (gemini.google.com) or Microsoft Copilot (copilot.microsoft.com). A free account is sufficient.
- The Project Manager Buddy GPT, used throughout the activities: https://chatgpt.com/g/g-hUcEmp6Rp-project-manager-buddy-project-management
- The course template pack (Excel, Word and PowerPoint templates used in the activities): https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1-FY7C8j7NfxiInKwSgK2c6hgTRT-8K73?usp=sharing
- Microsoft Excel, Word and PowerPoint (or Google Sheets, Docs and Slides) to complete the templates.
- No installation is required for any of the five course ed-tools — they all run in the browser.

**Set up before Activity 1**

- Sign in to your chosen GenAI tool and confirm you can start a new chat.
- Open the Project Manager Buddy GPT in a second browser tab and keep it open all course.
- Download the template pack and unzip it to a folder you can find quickly.
- Have a real project of your own in mind — one you know well enough to judge whether AI output is sensible.

**Working safely with GenAI at work**

Everything you put into a public GenAI tool may be used to improve that model. Before you use these techniques on real work, confirm what your organisation permits.

- Never paste client data, personal data, credentials or commercially sensitive information into a public tool.
- Your organisation may mandate an enterprise tool (such as Microsoft Copilot with commercial data protection) instead.
- Anonymise a scenario before prompting: replace real names, figures and identifiers with placeholders.
- Treat every figure, date, citation and source the model produces as unverified until you check it.
- Be transparent where GenAI materially shaped a deliverable you are handing to a client or sponsor.
- Keep the human in the loop — approval decisions stay with people, not models.

**Conventions used in every activity**

- Prompts you type into a GenAI tool are shown in a monospaced block.
- Placeholders such as [your project], [Industry] and [Project type] are replaced with your own details.
- Most activities are done in a group of 3–5; one person creates the shared ed-tool board and shares the link.
- Each activity ends with a 'Check your work' test — use it to confirm you have produced what was asked.
- Where an ed-tool cannot export a file, take a screenshot as your evidence.


## Reference — The Ten Prompting Patterns

These ten patterns are taught in Topic 1 and used throughout the course. Keep this section open during the activities; the pattern name tells you which shape of prompt to reach for.

**The anatomy of a good prompt**

- ROLE — who the AI should act as ('Act as an expert project manager in the construction industry').
- CONTEXT — your project, industry, constraints and what you already know.
- TASK — exactly what to produce, and how much of it.
- FORMAT — a table, a list, a document; the columns you want; the length.
- CHECK — instruct it to mark assumptions and cite sources so you can verify.


### Pattern — Persona

Frame the output from the perspective of a specific project role — or a non-human entity such as project management software.

**Example prompt**

```bash
Act as a stakeholder of the project. Perform an assessment of the project charter and provide a list of recommendations to improve scope clarity and completeness.
```


### Pattern — Question Refinement

Ask GenAI to improve your question when there is no clarity about the problem you are trying to solve.

**Example prompt**

```bash
Within the scope of creating a cost-benefit analysis of outsourcing (total cost: US$60,000) versus in-house hiring (total cost: US$70,000), suggest a better version of the question to be used.
```


### Pattern — Flipped Interaction

Let GenAI interview you — it asks the questions, one at a time, to find the gaps in your thinking.

**Example prompt**

```bash
I would like you to ask questions to help with my preparation for a project update with my stakeholders. Ask me questions one at a time so you can build the next question based on my responses.
```


### Pattern — Alternative Approaches

Generate several options, then compare their pros and cons before you decide.

**Example prompt**

```bash
I am a construction project manager experiencing a delay in completion of some tasks. I want to identify the root cause(s) of the problem. Suggest at least two options to deal with the problem(s) identified and compare the pros and cons of each. Present the results in a table.
```


### Pattern — Cognitive Verifier

Force GenAI to break a complex question into smaller sub-questions and then combine the answers.

**Example prompt**

```bash
A software development project has a US$100,000 budget and a timeline of six months. After three months the actual cost is US$60,000 and the project is 50% complete. What are the risks of not completing on time and on budget? Am I over or under budget? Am I behind or ahead of schedule? What is the earned value and estimate to complete? Combine the answers to produce the final answer to the first question.
```


### Pattern — Fact Check List

For outputs that rest on external information, require the facts to be listed separately with links to sources.

**Example prompt**

```bash
Generate a list of the 10 most common frequent-flier challenges in the airline industry based on facts. The set of facts should be inserted at a specific point in the output and correctly show links to sources.
```


### Pattern — Visualisation Generator

Have GenAI write the prompt for a text-to-image or diagram tool so you can visualise project information.

**Example prompt**

```bash
I want to generate a prompt that I can provide to a diagram tool to visualise the 10 most common sources of project scope creep for my project kickoff presentation.
```


### Pattern — Reflection

Ask for the reasoning and assumptions behind a recommendation — essential for risk and people decisions.

**Example prompt**

```bash
I need to provide feedback to a project team member who made a mistake with financial implications. Explain the reasoning and assumptions behind your recommendation on how to approach this meeting with them.
```


### Pattern — Risk Matrix Template

A long-form template that produces a complete, industry-specific risk matrix in one pass.

**Example prompt**

```bash
Imagine you are an expert project manager in the [Industry], focusing on [Project type] projects. Develop a comprehensive risk matrix. For each risk include: Risk name, Description, Level of impact (high/medium/low) and Likelihood (high/medium/low). Cover budget, schedule, technology, regulatory, resource, scope creep, stakeholder, data security, quality and external dependency categories. Output as a table.
```


### Pattern — Decision Making Template

Decompose a complex decision into smaller questions that together answer it.

**Example prompt**

```bash
Imagine you are an expert project manager in the [Industry] working on a [Project type] project. Break the complex question "[Complex question?]" into smaller questions that collectively answer it. Consider the range of outcomes, sources of resistance, risks and mitigation plans, and key milestones. Provide one-to-three-sentence answers to each, then ask me where I would like to go deeper.
```

## Topic 01 — Project Initiation and Planning with GenAI  (LO1 · K2, A1)

Charter · business case · stakeholders · scope · WBS · schedule · cost

**Key concepts**

- What a project is — A unique product, service or result; time-limited; drives change and enables value creation.
- GenAI as your planning co-pilot — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot draft charters, WBS, schedules and estimates in minutes — you stay accountable for the judgement.
- Ten prompting patterns — Persona, Question Refinement, Flipped Interaction, Alternative Approaches, Cognitive Verifier, Fact Check List, Visualisation Generator, Reflection and the risk/decision templates.
- Project Charter — Authorises the project, names the sponsor and PM, and fixes objectives, budget and success criteria.
- Business case & value — Cost-benefit analysis and a Business Model Canvas prove economic feasibility before work starts.
- Scope & requirements — Collect requirements, build the Requirements Traceability Matrix, write the scope statement, decompose into a WBS.
- Schedule — Define, sequence and estimate activities; build the network diagram and find the critical path.
- Cost — Analogous, parametric, bottom-up and three-point estimating; the cost baseline plus contingency and management reserves.


### Activity 1 — Prompting Patterns for Project Managers

Learning outcome: LO1 — Apply structured prompting patterns to produce usable project management output.

Goal: Work through the ten prompting patterns on a project of your own. You will see the same question answered badly by a vague prompt and well by a structured one, and you will keep a personal prompt library for the rest of the course.

**What you'll produce**

A personal prompt library of 10 tested prompts, each with the output it produced and your critique.   (Tools: ChatGPT · Claude · Gemini · Copilot · Project Manager Buddy GPT.)

**Activity package**

Open activities/activity-01-prompting-patterns-for-project-managers/. It contains the Markdown activity guide and PDF rendition, a prompt sheet, a detailed instruction manual and a formatted mock-data workbook.

**Core prompt**

```bash
Act as a stakeholder of the project. Perform an assessment of the project charter and provide a list of recommendations to improve scope clarity and completeness.
```

**Step-by-step**

1. Open your preferred GenAI tool (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Copilot) and sign in. Open the Project Manager Buddy GPT in a second tab: https://chatgpt.com/g/g-hUcEmp6Rp-project-manager-buddy-project-management
2. Choose a real project from your own workplace — one you know well enough to judge whether the AI output is sensible. Write down its objective, budget range and timeline in one sentence each.
3. Start with a deliberately vague prompt: 'Help me with my project.' Note how generic the answer is. This is your baseline.
4. Now apply the Persona pattern: 'Act as a stakeholder of the project. Perform an assessment of the project charter and provide a list of recommendations to improve scope clarity and completeness.' Compare against the baseline.
5. Apply the Flipped Interaction pattern so the AI interviews you: 'Ask me questions one at a time to prepare for a stakeholder update on [your project].' Answer at least five questions and note which gap it exposed.
6. Apply the Cognitive Verifier pattern to a complex question about your project — one that needs several sub-answers combined. Observe how it decomposes the question.
7. Apply the Fact Check List pattern to a question that depends on external industry data. Verify at least two of the sources it cites actually exist and say what it claims.
8. Record each prompt, the output and a one-line critique in your prompt library. Mark any output that was confidently wrong — this is the habit that protects you all course.

**Check your work**

> ✅  You have 10 prompts in your library, each with output and a critique, and you can state one concrete case where GenAI produced a plausible but wrong answer that you caught.

### Activity 2 — Business Case and Cost-Benefit Analysis with GenAI

Learning outcome: LO1 — Establish the economic feasibility of a project using GenAI-assisted analysis.

Goal: Before a project is authorised somebody has to prove it is worth doing. You will use GenAI to draft a cost-benefit analysis and a one-page business case, then validate the business model behind it on the Business Model Canvas Studio.

**What you'll produce**

A completed cost-benefit analysis, a one-page business case, and a validated 9-block Business Model Canvas.   (Tools: GenAI tool · Business Model Canvas Studio (alfredang.github.io/bcm) · Cost Benefit Analysis.xlsx · Business Case on a Page.docx.)

**Activity package**

Open activities/activity-02-business-case-and-cost-benefit-analysis-with-genai/. It contains the Markdown activity guide and PDF rendition, a prompt sheet, a detailed instruction manual and a formatted mock-data workbook.

**Core prompt**

```bash
List the one-off and recurring costs, and the quantified annual benefits, of [your project] over 3 years. Present the result as a table with a column explaining how each figure will be verified.
```

**Step-by-step**

1. Form a group of 3–5. Download 'Cost Benefit Analysis.xlsx' and 'Business Case on a Page.docx' from the course template pack.
2. Agree on a project scenario for your group — for example introducing a GenAI knowledge assistant for your customer service team.
3. Prompt GenAI: 'List the one-off and recurring costs, and the quantified annual benefits, of [your project] over 3 years. Present as a table with a column for how each figure would be verified.'
4. Challenge the output with the Fact Check List pattern — ask it to separate figures it knows from figures it assumed. Replace every assumed figure with your own estimate.
5. Enter the validated figures into Cost Benefit Analysis.xlsx and read off the payback period and net benefit.
6. Open the Business Model Canvas Studio at https://alfredang.github.io/bcm/ — one member clicks 'Create New Canvas', enters a name and canvas title, then shares the QR code or invite link so the rest of the group joins.
7. Prompt GenAI: 'Draft a Business Model Canvas for [your project]. Give me 2–3 sticky notes for each of the nine blocks: Key Partners, Key Activities, Key Resources, Value Propositions, Customer Relationships, Channels, Customer Segments, Cost Structure, Revenue Streams.'
8. Add the AI's suggestions as sticky notes in the matching blocks. Set each note's status to Draft. Use the (i) 'Show guidance' button on any block you are unsure about.
9. As a group, review every note. Change the status to Validate for notes you have evidence for and Confirmed for notes you are certain of. Delete anything the AI invented that you cannot support.
10. Complete Business Case on a Page.docx using your validated numbers and canvas, and share your findings with the class.

**Check your work**

> ✅  Your canvas has all nine blocks populated, every note carries a Draft/Validate/Confirmed status, and your business case states a payback period you can defend with your own figures.

### Activity 3 — Develop the Project Charter with GenAI

Learning outcome: LO1 — Develop project parameters with GenAI according to business requirements.

Goal: The charter authorises the project and gives the PM the right to apply resources. You will generate a full charter with GenAI and then correct it against what a charter must actually contain.

**What you'll produce**

An approved-quality Project Charter for your group's project.   (Tools: GenAI tool · Project Manager Buddy GPT · Project Charter Template.xlsx.)

**Activity package**

Open activities/activity-03-develop-the-project-charter-with-genai/. It contains the Markdown activity guide and PDF rendition, a prompt sheet, a detailed instruction manual and a formatted mock-data workbook.

**Core prompt**

```bash
Write a project charter for launching an internal knowledge-sharing platform. Include objectives, scope, risks, stakeholders and success criteria. Mark every assumption clearly.
```

**Step-by-step**

1. Stay in your group of 3–5 and open 'Project Charter Template.xlsx' from the template pack.
2. Prompt GenAI: 'Write a project charter for launching an internal knowledge-sharing platform. Include objectives, scope, risks, stakeholders, and success criteria.' — or substitute your own project.
3. Check the draft against the required charter contents: sponsor, project manager and key stakeholders named; project description with measurable objectives; business need and financial goals; summary milestone schedule; assumptions, constraints and boundaries; approval requirements and approved budget; success and exit criteria.
4. List every element the AI omitted or left vague. In practice it will usually invent a budget and skip exit criteria.
5. Prompt it again to fix precisely those gaps: 'Revise the charter. Add measurable success criteria, explicit exit criteria, and mark every figure you assumed with [ASSUMPTION].'
6. Replace each [ASSUMPTION] with your group's own figure, then fill in Project Charter Template.xlsx.
7. Use the Persona pattern to stress-test it: 'Act as the project sponsor. Review this charter and list the three questions you would ask before signing it.' Answer those three questions in your charter.
8. Share your charter with the class and state which part GenAI got most wrong.

**Check your work**

> ✅  Your charter names a sponsor and PM, has measurable objectives and explicit success AND exit criteria, and contains no unmarked AI-invented figures.

### Activity 4 — Identify and Map Stakeholders with GenAI

Learning outcome: LO1 — Identify stakeholders and plan engagement using GenAI-assisted analysis.

Goal: Projects fail on stakeholders more often than on technology. You will use GenAI to generate a stakeholder register, then map power against interest and decide an engagement strategy for each.

**What you'll produce**

A stakeholder register, a power/interest map and a Stakeholder Engagement Assessment Matrix.   (Tools: GenAI tool · Stakeholder Impact Over Influence Matrix.xlsx · Salience Model Template.pptx · Stakeholder Engagement Assessment Matrix.xlsx.)

**Activity package**

Open activities/activity-04-identify-and-map-stakeholders-with-genai/. It contains the Markdown activity guide and PDF rendition, a prompt sheet, a detailed instruction manual and a formatted mock-data workbook.

**Core prompt**

```bash
Identify potential stakeholders and their influence and interest levels for a new city-wide bike-sharing system. Explain the evidence needed to validate each rating.
```

**Step-by-step**

1. In your group, open 'Stakeholder Impact Over Influence Matrix.xlsx' from the template pack.
2. Prompt GenAI: 'Identify potential stakeholders and their influence/interest levels for a new city-wide bike-sharing system.' — or use your own project.
3. Add the stakeholders GenAI missed. It reliably finds obvious internal roles and misses regulators, unions, adjacent departments and the people who lose something if the project succeeds.
4. Plot each stakeholder on the matrix: X-axis = Influence, Y-axis = Interest. Agree the four quadrant strategies — manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor.
5. Now apply the Salience Model. Prompt: 'Assess each stakeholder for power, legitimacy and urgency, and classify them as definitive, dominant, dangerous, dependent, discretionary, demanding or dormant.'
6. Place each stakeholder into the correct salience category in Salience Model Template.pptx and reconcile any disagreement with your power/interest map.
7. Open 'Stakeholder Engagement Assessment Matrix.xlsx'. Prompt GenAI: 'List stakeholders for this project and recommend their current and desired engagement levels (Unaware, Resistant, Neutral, Supportive, Leading) with a matching engagement strategy for each.'
8. Mark C for current and D for desired against each stakeholder, and write the specific action that closes each gap. Share with the class.

**Check your work**

> ✅  Every stakeholder has a quadrant, a salience category, a current and desired engagement level, and one named action to close the gap.

### Activity 5 — Assign Responsibilities with the RACI Ed-Tool

Learning outcome: LO1 — Allocate project responsibilities unambiguously using a responsibility assignment matrix.

Goal: A RACI matrix ends the 'I thought you were doing it' conversation. You will build one collaboratively in a live browser tool, using GenAI to propose the first pass.

**What you'll produce**

A completed RACI matrix for your project, agreed by the whole group.   (Tools: RACI Collaborative Matrix (alfredang.github.io/raci) · GenAI tool.)

**Activity package**

Open activities/activity-05-assign-responsibilities-with-the-raci-ed-tool/. It contains the Markdown activity guide and PDF rendition, a prompt sheet, a detailed instruction manual and a formatted mock-data workbook.

**Core prompt**

```bash
For a project with these activities [paste] and these roles [paste], propose a RACI assignment. Give exactly one Accountable role per activity and explain any ambiguous accountability.
```

**Step-by-step**

1. One member of your group opens https://alfredang.github.io/raci/ and clicks 'Create new board'. Share the QR code or board URL from the 'Share' button so everyone joins.
2. Note the board arrives pre-seeded with a GenAI project example — roles Solution Architect, Platform Team, Security & Compliance, Data/Knowledge Owner and five activities. Rename these to match your own project by clicking each header.
3. Use '+ Add role' and '+ Add activity' to complete your grid. Aim for 4–6 roles and 6–8 activities.
4. Prompt GenAI: 'For a project with these activities [paste] and these roles [paste], propose a RACI assignment. Give exactly one Accountable per activity and explain any activity where accountability is ambiguous.'
5. Drag the A, R and C markers into the cells to match the AI's proposal. Note this tool uses A/R/C only — there is no Informed marker, so record 'informed' parties in your communications plan instead.
6. Now audit it as a group. Check the two rules that matter: exactly ONE Accountable per activity, and no role that is Accountable for everything. Click a marker to remove it, or drag it to the trash.
7. Find at least one place where the AI's suggestion was wrong for your organisation and fix it. Ask why it got it wrong — usually it assumes a generic org structure.
8. Screenshot the finished board (the tool has no export) and share it with the class.

**Check your work**

> ✅  Every activity row has exactly one A, at least one R, and your group can justify each assignment; you have a screenshot saved as evidence.

## Topic 03 — Managing Project Risks with GenAI  (LO3 · K1, A3, A4)

Risk · procurement · monitoring & control · earned value · closure

**Key concepts**

- Risk vs issue — Risks are future and may be positive or negative (risk register); issues are present and always negative (issue log).
- Identify and analyse risk — RBS, brainstorming, SWOT and the Delphi technique; qualitative probability/impact then quantitative analysis.
- Quantitative analysis — Sensitivity (tornado) charts and decision-tree expected monetary value.
- Plan risk responses — Avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept — escalate, exploit, share, enhance; secondary and residual risk.
- Procurement — RFI/RFP/RFQ, contract types, claims and alternative dispute resolution.
- Monitor and control — Integrated change control, validate and control scope, schedule and cost.
- Earned value analysis — PV, EV, AC; CV, SV, CPI, SPI; EAC and ETC forecasting.
- Control quality & close — Check sheets, control charts and the rule of seven; then close the project and capture lessons learned.

4. Now find the opposite: which risks did YOUR team identify that the AI missed? These are almost always the organisation-specific ones, and they are usually the ones that actually materialise.
5. Use the Risk Breakdown Structure as a checklist to find further gaps — work down from high-level categories (External, Organisational, Technical, Project Management) to specific risks.
6. For each risk, confirm it is written as a proper risk statement: cause → risk event → effect. 'The vendor may be late' is not enough; 'Because the vendor has one integration engineer, their resource may be unavailable in December, delaying UAT by two weeks' is.
7. Distinguish risks from issues as you go: risks are future and may be positive or negative and go in the risk register; issues are present, always negative, and go in the issue log.
8. Assign an owner to every risk and share your register with the class.

**Check your work**

> ✅  Your register has risks from both sources, every entry is written as cause → event → effect with an owner, and you can name one risk your team found that the AI missed.

### Activity 20 — Quantitative Risk Analysis with GenAI — Tornado Charts and Decision Trees

Learning outcome: LO3 — Quantify risk exposure to support a financial decision.

Goal: When a decision carries real money, qualitative scoring is not enough. You will run a sensitivity analysis and a decision tree, using GenAI for the setup and doing the arithmetic yourself.

**What you'll produce**

A tornado chart identifying your most sensitive risk variables and a decision tree with expected monetary values.   (Tools: GenAI tool · Tornado Chart - Sensitivity Analysis.xlsx · Decision Tree Analysis.xlsx.)

**Activity package**

Open activities/activity-20-quantitative-risk-analysis-with-genai-tornado-charts/. It contains the Markdown activity guide and PDF rendition, a prompt sheet, a detailed instruction manual and a formatted mock-data workbook.

**Core prompt**

```bash
Use the supplied high, base and low values to rank the ten variables by their effect on project cost or schedule. Produce tornado-chart data and one decision tree with expected monetary values, showing every calculation.
```

**Step-by-step**

1. In your group, open 'Tornado Chart - Sensitivity Analysis.xlsx'.
2. Prompt GenAI: 'List 10 risk variables that could affect the cost or schedule of a digital transformation project. Estimate high and low values for each.'
3. Enter each variable with its high/low range and plot the bars, longest at the top. The variables at the top of the tornado are where your management attention belongs.
4. Sanity-check the ranges. GenAI produces symmetric ranges by default; real project risks are usually asymmetric — things go wrong further than they go right. Widen the downside where your experience says so.
5. Open 'Decision Tree Analysis.xlsx'. Define a real either/or decision from your project — build vs buy, or two vendors.
6. Prompt GenAI: 'Structure this decision as a decision tree. For each branch give the cost, the possible outcomes with their probabilities, and the payoff of each outcome.'
7. Calculate the Expected Monetary Value for each branch yourself: EMV = sum of (probability x payoff) for each outcome, minus the branch cost. Do NOT accept the AI's arithmetic — verify every multiplication.
8. Compare your computed EMV with GenAI's. Where they differ, find the error. This is the single most important habit when using AI for quantitative work.
9. State your recommended decision and the EMV that supports it. Share with the class.

**Check your work**

> ✅  Your tornado chart is ordered by sensitivity with justified asymmetric ranges, and you have hand-verified the EMV of every decision tree branch.

### Activity 21 — Plan Risk Responses with GenAI

Learning outcome: LO3 — Devise risk mitigation measures to ensure project success (A4).

Goal: A risk register with no responses is a list of things you will be surprised by. You will plan a response for every priority risk and check for the second-order effects that responses create.

**What you'll produce**

A response plan for every priority risk, with owners, triggers and fallback plans.   (Tools: GenAI tool · your risk register from Activities 18-19.)

**Activity package**

Open activities/activity-21-plan-risk-responses-with-genai/. It contains the Markdown activity guide and PDF rendition, a prompt sheet, a detailed instruction manual and a formatted mock-data workbook.

**Core prompt**

```bash
For each priority risk [paste], recommend one primary response strategy from avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept, escalate, exploit, share or enhance. Give the action, owner, trigger, fallback and response cost.
```

**Step-by-step**

1. Confirm the eight response strategies. For threats: Avoid, Transfer, Mitigate, Accept. For opportunities: Exploit, Share, Enhance, Accept. Escalate applies to both when the risk is outside your authority.
2. Prompt GenAI: 'For each of these priority risks [paste], recommend a response strategy from avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept, escalate, exploit, share or enhance. Give the specific action, an owner role, the trigger condition, and the estimated cost of the response.'
3. Audit the strategy names. GenAI routinely labels a mitigation as an avoidance — avoiding a risk means eliminating the cause entirely, usually by changing the plan, not by reducing the likelihood.
4. Check the cost logic: a response costing more than the risk exposure it removes is a bad response. Prompt: 'For each response, compare its cost against the risk exposure (probability x impact). Flag any response that costs more than the exposure.'
5. Identify SECONDARY risks — new risks created by your responses. Prompt: 'What new risks does each of these responses introduce?' Transferring risk to a vendor, for instance, creates vendor dependency risk.
6. Identify RESIDUAL risk — what remains after the response is implemented. Every accepted residual risk needs a contingency plan and a contingency reserve.
7. Write a fallback plan for your top three risks: what you do if the primary response fails.
8. Define the trigger condition for each response — the observable signal that tells you to act. A response with no trigger is never implemented in time.
9. Update your register and share your top three responses with their triggers and fallbacks.

**Check your work**

> ✅  Every priority risk has a correctly-named strategy, an owner, an observable trigger, an identified secondary and residual risk, and your top three have fallback plans.

### Activity 23 — Earned Value Analysis with GenAI

Learning outcome: LO3 — Measure project performance objectively and forecast the outcome.

Goal: Earned value is the one technique that tells you objectively whether you are ahead or behind, over or under. You will compute it by hand first, then test whether GenAI gets it right.

**What you'll produce**

A complete earned value analysis with variances, indices and a forecast.   (Tools: GenAI tool · calculator.)

**Activity package**

Open activities/activity-23-earned-value-analysis-with-genai/. It contains the Markdown activity guide and PDF rendition, a prompt sheet, a detailed instruction manual and a formatted mock-data workbook.

**Core prompt**

```bash
Using the supplied baseline, completion and actual-cost data, calculate PV, EV, AC, CV, SV, CPI, SPI, EAC and ETC. Show the formula and substitution for every result, then interpret the project status.
```

**Step-by-step**

1. Work this classic scenario by hand, individually. Task: develop and install ten printer drivers. Budget: $100,000 ($10K per driver). Time: 10 weeks (1 driver per week). At week 5: 4 drivers are complete and $47,500 has been spent.
2. Compute the three base values. PV = the budget for work SCHEDULED by now. EV = the budget for work ACTUALLY DONE. AC = what has actually been spent.
3. Confirm your answers: PV = $50,000 (5 drivers scheduled x $10K). EV = $40,000 (4 drivers done x $10K). AC = $47,500.
4. Compute the variances. Cost Variance CV = EV - AC = $40,000 - $47,500 = -$7,500 (negative is bad — over budget). Schedule Variance SV = EV - PV = $40,000 - $50,000 = -$10,000 (negative is bad — behind schedule).
5. Compute the indices. CPI = EV / AC = 40,000 / 47,500 = 0.84. SPI = EV / PV = 40,000 / 50,000 = 0.80. Both below 1 — over budget and behind schedule.
6. Forecast the outcome. EAC = BAC / CPI = 100,000 / 0.84 = approximately $119,000. ETC = EAC - AC. State what you would tell the sponsor.
7. NOW test GenAI. Give it the same scenario and ask for PV, EV, AC, CV, SV, CPI, SPI and EAC. Compare every figure against yours.
8. Where it differs, identify the error. GenAI commonly confuses EV with AC, or computes EAC without using CPI. This is precisely why you must be able to do this by hand.
9. Discuss as a group: the assessment is open book, but the arithmetic is yours. What is the minimum you must be able to compute without help?

**Check your work**

> ✅  You can compute PV, EV, AC, CV, SV, CPI, SPI and EAC unaided for a fresh scenario, and you found at least one error in the AI's version.

## Applying This at Work

**Start with one artifact**

Do not try to change everything at once. Pick a single artifact you produce regularly — a status report, a risk register or a set of meeting minutes — and draft the next one with GenAI. Compare how long it took against your usual approach, and note what you had to correct.

**Reuse your prompt library**

The prompt library you built in Activity 1 is the most portable thing you take from this course. Every prompt in it has already been tested on a project you understand. Keep adding to it, and record the critique alongside each prompt so you remember what the model tends to get wrong.

**The five checks that catch most GenAI errors**

- Arithmetic — recompute every number yourself. Earned value, EMV, PERT and critical path are all commonly wrong.
- Sources — if it cites a source, open it. Invented citations are common and look entirely plausible.
- Assumptions — ask it to mark every figure it assumed rather than knew, then replace those figures.
- Completeness — ask it what it left out. It reliably omits project management effort, testing, training and handover.
- Context — it knows nothing about your organisation's politics, history or constraints. That gap is where you add value.

**Where GenAI should not go**

- Decisions about a specific named person — performance, discipline or selection.
- Anything involving client data, personal data or commercially sensitive figures in a public tool.
- Final approval of a baseline, a budget or a contract — those remain human decisions.
- Any output you would not be willing to defend as your own work.

## Glossary

- **Generative AI (GenAI)** — Tools that create new text, images, code or ideas from a prompt by predicting likely output from patterns in training data.
- **Prompt** — The instruction you give a GenAI tool. A structured prompt states role, context, task, format and how to flag assumptions.
- **Hallucination** — Fluent, confident output that is factually wrong — most often an invented figure, source or citation.
- **Project Charter** — The document that authorises the project, names the sponsor and PM, and fixes objectives, budget and success criteria.
- **Business Case** — A documented economic feasibility study establishing the benefits that justify the project work.
- **Stakeholder** — Anyone affected by the project, or who believes they are affected.
- **SEAM** — Stakeholder Engagement Assessment Matrix — records each stakeholder's Current and Desired engagement level.
- **RACI** — A responsibility assignment matrix: Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed. Exactly one Accountable per activity.
- **RTM** — Requirements Traceability Matrix — traces each requirement from its source through to its test case and deliverable.
- **WBS** — Work Breakdown Structure — the decomposition of project scope into assignable work packages.
- **Work Package** — A piece of work small enough to be assigned to one person or team.
- **Critical Path** — The sequence of activities with zero float that determines the shortest possible project duration.
- **Float** — Free float is how long an activity can slip without delaying its successor; total float without delaying the project.
- **PERT** — A three-point estimate weighting the most likely value: (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6.
- **Cost Baseline** — The approved time-phased budget: work package costs plus the contingency reserve.
- **Contingency Reserve** — Budget held inside the cost baseline for identified risks. Controlled by the project manager.
- **Management Reserve** — Budget held outside the cost baseline for unforeseen in-scope work. Requires management approval.
- **Risk vs Issue** — A risk is a future event that may be positive or negative (risk register); an issue is a present, always-negative problem (issue log).
- **Secondary Risk** — A new risk created directly by implementing a risk response.
- **Residual Risk** — The risk that remains after a response has been implemented, and must be knowingly accepted.
- **Trigger Condition** — The observable signal that a risk is developing and the response should be implemented.
- **EMV** — Expected Monetary Value — the sum of (probability × payoff) across a decision branch's outcomes.
- **Earned Value (EV)** — The budget for the work actually completed, expressed in budget terms.
- **CPI / SPI** — Cost and Schedule Performance Index. EV÷AC and EV÷PV. Above 1 is good; below 1 is bad.
- **EAC / ETC** — Estimate At Completion (BAC÷CPI) and Estimate To Complete (EAC−AC).
- **Rule of Seven** — On a control chart, seven consecutive points on one side of the mean — or seven rising or falling — indicates a trend to investigate.
- **Integrated Change Control** — The process of raising, impact-analysing, logging, approving and communicating a change to a baseline.
- **Scope Creep** — Unapproved scope added outside change control. Gold plating is delivering extras the customer never asked for.
